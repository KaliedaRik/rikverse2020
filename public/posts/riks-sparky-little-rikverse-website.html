My name is Rik and I have been committing and abusing poetry for most of my life.

My first encounters with poetry were seemingly innocent. I learned to recite some 'Mother Goose' verse before I learned about school. Then as I discovered alphabets and reading I dabbled in Mr Milne's classics: 'When We Were Very Young', and 'Now We Are Six' – John had / Great Big / Waterproof Boots on; / John had a / Great Big / Waterproof Hat; / John had a / Great Big / Waterproof Macintosh- / And that / (Said John) / Is that.

... Apologies. I can't help it. These words are seductive!

I think I was nine when I first moved on to some stronger verse. 'Old Possum's Book of Practical Cats' came from the hand of a man who went by the street name of T. S. Eliot. By mixing quotable verse with feline cunning that Dante sniffer shook up a potent cocktail of melodies and rhymes: a classic fix of gateway strophes. I only learned much later how dangerous – toxic – Eliot's poetry could be: beats and visions to bend the mind around corners and through wormholes. Seriously, when you sit with Eliot, check that the others in the circle are solid!

My teachers noticed that something was amiss. At that time the most popular treatments were poetry dissection courses or, in more severe cases like mine, forced microdosing – repeated sessions to memorise Wordsworth's 'Daffodils', in the hope that the banality of that poem could drive out the addiction to stronger stuff.

The intervention worked for a while, until I left school and "life happened". At its best I could go for weeks, months even, without even thinking about poetry.

But it's still there. Poetry is pernicious; it never goes away. If it's not the song lyrics that trip you up, then it'll be the jingles that dribble past the ear muffs and into the ancient reptile regions of your brain stem: John had / Great Big / Waterproof Boots on ...
