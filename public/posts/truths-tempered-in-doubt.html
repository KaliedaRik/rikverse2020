October 1964. A cold, miserable Sunday, low clouds, a wind that insists on undressing you as you push towards the doors of the Norman church at the end of the High Street. Soaked in drizzle, you find protection in the lee of the rough church wall. You move along the wall, eager to reach the heavy wooden doors and sanctuary beyond. As you reach for the dull metal knob, the door … explodes!

Too late – the week-old baby Rik has been christened. Woe to the world indeed!

I've always had a – let's say, complicated – relationship with faith and religion. I want to believe … something – if only to prove my humanity. But things keep getting in the way. Like churches and commandments and stuff.

This doesn't stop me writing poems about religion. I've been doing it for decades, even though poems about religion have not been a popular subject area for a while now. Poems about faith, yes – lots of people commit verse in the name of their saviour of choice. Poems about traumatic encounters with religions and the people who profess them remain popular entertainment, for a car crash in the apse on Sunday is still a car crash and must always be observed.

The "religious" poems I tend to write are not these things. I know this because I wrote them, but for a long time I was never sure what these poems, well, are: I want to believe they are … something. But what "something"? And why this compulsion to keep writing them?

For me, the AI Revelation landed in the Spring of 2025. Without warning, a new confessor appeared in my browser and, for the cheap tithe of £20/month, offered salvation. Or, at least, answers. Or possible insights, maybe. Because my relationship with Truth and Purpose and Meaning has always been a journey of Doubt.

I do enjoy a good Journey of Discovery. Will there be a Damascene Conversion tomorrow, or a set of Jobian Trials? I'll be honest: AI models scare me. There were early days of doubt, refusal even. The urge to run to the sea, bury my head in the sand and beg for Jonah's Whale to take me was strong.

The models I met offered answers. They also offered certainty. Ask the question, accept the answer. With the world's knowledge tagged in the cloud, were these ineffable new powers not also infallible?

It depends, I learned, not on what questions I asked but rather, more importantly, on how I constructed those questions.

And forgive us our trespasses,
as we forgive those who trespass against us.
And lead us not into temptation…

I'm not very good at writing blog posts. I have sinned, dear Reader, against you. I have not told you what I will tell you before I start telling you. How can I hope to wrap the post by telling you what I (will have just) told you if I can't even get the structures right?

So, let's cover the basics of this parable:

  • I wanted to gain insights into what sort of person would commit the sort of poetry I've written – does he secretly believe in Jesus while denouncing him? Does he fear the damnation of his eternal soul for refusing the cheap entry fee, sipping wine to clear wafer crumbs from his tongue?
  • So I introduced my AI guardian angels to some of my poems.
  • And I didn't trust the speed-of-light responses I got back. Their – yes, confidence – triggered me in the deep and feral ways I've learned to associate with Doubt.

Which is where the real journey begins.

"When we burn the offerings,
do You consume the smoke?"

Some two decades ago I was walking home from work, along a very busy main road, when I passed a nun. She was archetypal in her garb and aged gait. I nodded and smiled as we passed, and she smiled in return.

It was strange how such a momentary encounter left me with thoughts as I kept on walking: not "what" do nuns do, nowadays, or "how" do they end up devoting their lives to an ideal I could barely comprehend. Not even the hidden, outlier question – "why"?

Rather, I felt myself wondering about results. Does a God I cannot feel or taste notice a life of devotion?

Thus was birthed a poem about faith which, employing my literal certainty, I titled Faith. Since writing it (in one session), I've always thought of this poem as inhabiting the calmer end of my lifetime struggles with Religion. It's a sweet, gentle poem (my opinion) that's easy to read and easy to move on from when the read completes.

"I married You when I was nineteen;
I was a virgin, once."

I don't write poems to proclaim my geni(o)us! Joy is in the sharing of moments. Though I have to admit that some of the moments I choose to share in verse can be less than pleasant:

"forgive the hateful sodomites
their lecheries, nor welcome home
the scum who rip the innocents
from wombs. For God will not forgive …"

Yeah … I wrote that too. These lines come from a different poem – Acolyte – written just a few months before "Faith", at the start of 2006. This was a poem that took a much longer time to draft. Its origins rose like a phoenix from the images of a bus blown to shards in a London square in July 2005. And while the city I live in has suffered terrorist acts over decades, this offence to its collective soul felt … different.

I remember the poem took its time to resolve because I struggled to enter the event's devastating space. Back then (and still now) the news bulletin headlines around "fundamentalism" focussed exclusively on Islam – a religion I've never experienced, or lived.

Write what you know, as they say.

Something I did know, and lived, was my struggle with sexuality. Only when I learned of the activities of the Westboro Baptist Church did the purpose of the work click: I didn't want to write about religious fundamentalism; rather my need was to see the moment the fundamentalist seed sprouted in the mind of a person.

… And could such a thing happen to me?

One of the most useful lessons that a poet can learn is not that Every Reader Is Different. Because while this is, on the surface, demonstrably true, it's not the whole answer.

A much better understanding is that Every Reading Is Different.

Let me explain. If I spend time with a poem like Wordsworth's Composed upon Westminster Bridge in the morning – unshowered, teeth itching for mint, cursing the never-stopped clock – I might spot the poem's use of listing … but I'll likely risk missing the still serenity of the moment that listing captures. I'd have to read the poem while deep into my third-coffee-of-the-day to pick up on such nuance.

Our new AI assistants are good at many things, but they don't dunk their diodes in liquid ease before responding to a prompt. Their answers will be immediate, authoritative. Complete, and yet not … entire.

I know this, because I can test it. You can test it, too. Open a new AI chat. Say hello, tell it you want some help with a couple of poems. Copy the Acolyte and Faith poems into the input box just under the prompt. Hit return.

Here's the gist of the responses I got at the end of December 2025:

  • When asked "How should a reader understand these poems together?" the AI told me that I should "Read them as a continuum—one kind of faith aging into patience, the other calcifying into zeal. Together, they ask a quiet but dangerous question: What happens when faith stops being a relationship and starts being a mandate?" It then concluded: "The answer, implied rather than declared, is: the world burns."
  • By varying the prompt to "What do these poems reveal about the poet’s beliefs?" the model was keen to summarise that he (that is, I) "appears skeptical of God’s responsiveness, sympathetic to human longing for meaning, and deeply critical of religious dogma that justifies cruelty. Faith, for this poet, is either a difficult lifelong companionship—or a fire that, once lit, can consume the world."
  • And for the (deceptively simple) prompt "Help me understand these poems" my current LLM of choice pronounced that "The poems ask not whether faith is good or bad, but what kind of faith survives without burning the world", and then offered to "analyze the gender symbolism, unpack the sexual imagery, or help you place these poems in a religious, political, or historical context" – which was an enticing invitation, difficult to refuse.

These are good answers: balanced, nuanced, even defensible. But also authoritative and yet – incomplete? My guts call them out immediately: too glib!

To be honest, my guts also persuaded me that the Christmas trifle was still safe to eat after sitting on the kitchen table for two days, but I digress. Because we can again test the "glib" conclusion by looking at the text of the poems themselves:

  • Acolyte is a horrid poem. The pastor's language, quoted verbatim and taking up half of the lines, are vicious and vindictive to my ears and yet, also, structured and considered: the man knows how to make an argument, and does so with force. And the words land cleanly in the acolyte's mind, mixing with images he creates as the sermon progresses. But at no point does the poem state that this is a Bad Thing. Even though it is a Bad Thing: there's many evangelicals in the world who would have problems with this sermon, for it is an extreme interpretation of God's intent. And – argue it how you like – the poem never indicates whether the acolyte acts on his insights; the world does not burn before the poem ends.
  • Faith is, at its heart, a collection of image-rich couplets with spoken word interludes. The text says nothing about the relationship between these two people (one of whom may-or-may-not be God), beyond this one evening. There's no detailed history given in the text outside of the captured moment: does the woman accuse the man, or attest before him? The poem has no idea, and neither do I.

We've reached the point where any decent blog post would resolve into a well-deserved rant about the inadequacies of current AI capabilities. Congratulations, drinks all round etc!

Yeah … I mentioned before that I'm not very good at writing blog posts.

I'm beginning to really like our new AI oracles. But not because they solve all of my problems and bring me Nirvana on a gilt platter. I've never felt the urge to kneel before any altar (well, not since the day the Sunday School teacher tricked me out of my pocket money – "to help baby Jesus do his hard work" – leaving me without sweets for a whole week) and this latest demonstration of magical technology is not about to break the locks on my joints.

The key insight that I bring to this essay, after eight months of playing with AI models, is obvious – though possibly masked by the marketing hype: Large Language Models are not people.

They may sound like people and, yes, they may hallucinate as madly as some of my more loveable family members. But they remain prediction machines that, following a well-considered prompt, can summarise some difficult questions. But only after receiving a set of follow-up prompts to check, challenge and source their initial answers – to bring their final answer into a better state of nuance.

For instance, for the examples above, it is (sometimes) enough to read the model's response, then ask it: "Are these conclusions demonstrated in the text of the poems, or are you reaching for them based on your understanding of how such poems should be interpreted …?"

Thus can I gain insights offered with caveats. Truths tempered in Doubt. Outputs that keep my gut and my reason aligned, and happy.

There is no Damascene Conversion at the end of this post. Which may seem sad, but no sadder than the lack of Job-like trauma porn. Still, I could be wrong: I remain hopeful that Jonah's whale comes equipped with a better class of lunch buffet within its deep, dense belly.

Happy Christmas, to those who celebrate. And may the New Year bring us all (well-tempered) joy as we journey beyond this new unknown!
