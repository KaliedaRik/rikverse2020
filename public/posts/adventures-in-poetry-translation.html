Sometimes I want to read poetry that originates in languages other than my own. Because some of the greatest poets that the 20th century has known weren't born speaking English. Federico García Lorca, for instance, or Constantine Cavafy.

Which is when I hit a wall. I don't speak Spanish, or Greek. Of course, I can say things like "Thank you!" and "Can I have the bill, please?" – knowing the pleasantries in the language of the country you're visiting is important, if only to distinguish myself from most other tourists drifting through locations, all of us collecting our pilgrim badges.

I knew nothing of Lorca before this year, except for some rumours that he was a great Spanish poet who died in terrible circumstances during General Franco's conquest of Spain. When I discovered his fate, and the likely reasons for that fate, I wanted to know more of him. When I stumbled into the history of some of his most intimate poems – those secretive Sonetos del amor oscuro – I needed to read them, know them. Consume them!

But there was an issue ...

Translating is a hard problem. While modern technologies have improved to the point where they can generate "general" (coarse-grained) translations of text – even real-time speech – on the fly, translating literature is different. Literature has nuance, it has authorial intent. It often carries a deep knowledge of its language, and a set of home-grown tools – phrase patterns, soundscapes, white space – to manipulate the local reader in specific, disturbing ways. Poems, in particular, are tricksters.

I read some translations of Lorca's sonnets. Some read beautifully in English; others were less elegant – and yet in their strangeness they pointed to thoughts and passions that the "better" translations seemed to miss. This intrigued me: what's going on?

It turns out that there's a (somewhat civil) debate going on in translation circles around what purpose a translation should serve, and how things should be surfaced in the translation. Figures [warning: name-drop avalanche!] such as Hans Vermeer and his Skopos (purpose) theory bump heads with Lawrence Venuti railing against the "invisible translator", and Gayatri Spivak fighting to give the subaltern space in the debate. Even ghosts have their say: Ezra Pound, Robert Lowell, Anne Carson – none refuse to stay silent.

Which leads to wide variations in the English translations of a given poem. In how many ways can Cavafy's Η Πόλις be rendered into English? Many ways, it turns out, and each making compromises which alter the reading experience.

I read those translations of The City that I could find online. Some simple; others eerie in their almost-English vibe. In each I caught glimpses of The Great Writer himself; reading them together offered me a blurred view of the man between the cracks - a shattered collage of a mind needing to tell me something ... but what?

I needed more. I needed my own translation of these poems. I needed to hunt down these dead visionaries and beat confessions from their texts. I needed to learn Greek, to a high proficiency, now!

My name is Rik, and I am not a translator. I do not speak any foreign languages beyond their most basic pleasantries. I struggle to learn languages – I failed my French exam twice, probably because after the first week's lessons I decided, in my eleven-year-old certainty, that I could invent a better language myself.

So this post is not about my thoughts or stance in the Great Ongoing Debate about the hows and whys of poetry translation. I don't have a position, because I haven't earned that right. Instead I'll nod my head and agree with everybody then, in typical fashion, go and do my own thing in the corner.

This is a post about how I approached the translation of Great Poems by Great Poets, using the only tools I had available: Google Translate; various AI large language models; and an English poet's intuition about what I wanted to get from the exercise: to hug duende-hot Lorca, and offer cool Cavafy a welcome, inviting nod.

But not a technical post. No obscurity here! More like one of those recipes where the pre-ramble is much longer than the "follow these steps" part. Maybe I'll include a paragraph on how to present the results at the end. In true blog post fashion, we'll see how the writing of the bits before the end goes.

Baked Cavafy presque Naturelle

Follow this easy-to-replicate recipe to generate bespoke translations of foreign poems to meet your everyday needs.

Ingredients

  • One Cavafy poem - for best results select from the official Cavafy archive.
  • Access to Google Translate.
  • Large language models – one each of: Gemini Pro; ChatGPT (or vary to taste requirements).
  • An electronic notepad of some sort. Note that the quality of the software will not impact on the quality of the result.
  • A sense of adventure.

Preparation time: 1-2 hours.
Cooking time: 1-2 days.
Rest for: 1-2 weeks.
Serving: garnish with 'After' references, for clarity.

Preparation

1. Take the original poem, including the title, and feed it through Google Translate. Copy the original text, and the results, into the notepad.

This may seem like an amateur action. Asking a generalist tool like Google Translate to provide the initial translation is – literally! – asking for a poor translation. But that's what I needed for my starting point: a very broad-brush, generalist translation, complete with a lack of nuance or ear for cadence.

The alternative is to start from one of the canonical translations. But in my view such an approach risks importing another translator's preconceptions and preferences into the exercise, which is not what I want: this is my translation, however poor the end product may turn out to be.

2. Make a copy of the original poem and split each word onto its own line. Sort the lines into alphabetical order. Go through the resulting list and remove duplicate entries. Add a blank line between each entry. Feed this list into Google Translate and copy both list and results into the notepad. Then copy-paste to match each translation with its original word.

3. Repeat step 2, but this time breaking the original text into (what appear to be) phrases.

This is where the work begins, by building a dictionary. Languages are tricky things, and we need to get to a fine grain to begin picking out the nuances and gotchas in the original text.

4. Copy the original text to the bottom of the notepad, then separate each line with additional blank lines. Using the dictionary, prepare a transliteration of each line, word for word.

5. The results of step 4 will be messy. Now look at the phrase dictionary and, beneath the transliteration lines, compose a line of the likely meaning for each phrase and line.

This may seem like a lot of work. It probably is too much work. But these steps gave me an approximation of how the original language is setting up its words and phrases, and possible issues I was going to face when the cooking (real translation work) started. I also found that the transliteration began sketching out the mind I was seeking – a rough outline, granted, but still a target I could bring into focus.

Cooking

6. Start a new chat with your main large language model. Ask for its help to explain a poem to you. Then feed in the original text (including the title) and consider the LLM's response.

I discovered quite quickly, during early translation exercises, that the LLMs available in the second half of 2025 were very knowledgeable about well-known foreign poems – particularly those by Lorca and Cavafy – and could do a reasonable job of explaining the poem in easy-to-understand terms. There is a huge number of academic papers about these poems (and these poets) in the LLM training data.

7. Now ask the LLM to explain some of the difficulties translators faced when translating the poem into English.

Again, LLMs have a good understanding of the approaches to translating well-known poems, and the critiques that various (often canonical) translations had already received. They can quickly summarise the (many) pitfalls that anyone who wants to create a new translation must navigate around, and the choices that need to be made.

But this is also the point where I learned that every LLM in existence thinks that it, too, is a poet. Stern words often have to be added to the chat to prevent any attempt at versification.

8. Marinade – overnight if necessary, in a cool, dark place – the first Google Translation result with the transliteration exercise results, alongside the responses the LLM generated about the poem and its particular translation difficulties.

This is the place where I confronted the realities of translation. Trade-offs have to be made, for no translation can match the structures and sonics, the metaphors and allusions, the pacing and layout, and the intent of the original work. It simply cannot be done! Some things needed to be abandoned and, for me, the simplest thing to abandon was any rhyme schemes present in the original.

Once that decision was made, others fell in place quite easily. I chose to keep the placement of metaphors and allusions as close as possible to the original: Cavafy and Lorca both had their reasons, and it was those reasons I wanted to see. And while some metaphors were easy to translate into English, others became nonsensical when cleaving too hard to transliteration; this is where I could allow myself to be "inspired" by the original to create my own equivalent metaphors.

I also decided to keep to the visual structure of the original poems. No combining lines or splitting lines. Even if the results read bad, I was determined they should look good!

9. Write the initial translation. Do this on paper (or a whiteboard, or a tide-washed beach), with a writing implement of your choice. Type the final version of this initial attempt into the notepad once you are happy with the result.

10. Open a new chat window with the LLM, or a different LLM. Instruct it that you are writing a translation of a poem. Feed in the original text, then your initial translation. Read the response, then walk away from the keyboard and give yourself half an hour's quiet time to calm down and retrofit your mind to accept the critique in a more proactive manner.

This is the step that stung the most, for me. Because it was the moment where, for every poem, I had to be made to see that my initial understanding of the poem was often some distance from the original poet's intent.

As the adage goes: I am not as good as I think I am.

Note also that the LLM will attempt to offer its own translation as part of its response. This seems to be an inevitable rule of every LLM's "helpfulness" guidance: it must be resisted at all costs! One time, I had to admonish Google's Gemini in harsh tones, threatening to drive an industrial magnet into the middle of its data centre if it generated another unwanted "translation" of its own. To summarise, the best solution seems to be to ask the LLM to assume the role of an academic specialising in poetry translation before requesting the critique.

11. Iterate, several times. Take on board the points the LLM makes on each draft and consider them: use the ones that feel most useful to your evolving vision of what the translation should be, and do; discard suggestions that pull away from that vision.

This was, for me, the most interesting and absorbing part of the translation exercise. This is where I got to "see", rather than "know", my target poets:

  • For the Lorca translations, this is where I began to get a gut feeling for his passion and his concept of duende. Translating duende, which is so quintessentially Andalusian, into English became my overriding concern because duende is the root of Lorca's dark sonnets and, without ingesting that drive I could never meet Lorca.
  • But for Cavafy, an entirely different set of requirements came to the fore. The need for a clerical coldness became paramount, for Cavafy is the master of juxtaposition: he expected his readers to work alongside him in each of his poems, to see what he was showing them. Many conversations with the LLM about heat maps ensued.

12. Know when to stop iterating. When the translation meets your needs, when the mind behind the original text comes into focus – stop.

There came a point where I was looking at the impact of tweaking individual words, at punctuation decisions … which bored me. There came iterations where most of the points made by the LLM seemed – redundant. Or the LLM started to gush with overt praise: a telltale sign that the chat was beginning to turn stale and a new chat, often with a different LLM, needed to be opened.

These are all indications that the cooking is done. To continue at this point would be to overthink the exercise (burn the buns). When the work became boring, I allowed myself to stop. Because no translation is perfect, and perfection was never the reason for me to attempt the translation in the first place.

This has become one of my longer blog posts. I'll not apologise for its length.

Before this past Summer, I had never thought of myself as a translator. I still don't. Rather, I've learned that I am capable of taking a non-English poem, written by a poet who interests me, and bringing it into a form that I can read, enjoy, learn from.

I've come to appreciate a set of poems that Federico Lorca hid from all but his closest friends – not because he was ashamed of poems which he considered to be some of his best work, but rather for how they could shame him. He was right to be fearful. Before this year I had barely heard of him; now my heart screams for his memory.

And Constantine Cavafy? What an extraordinary talent I have come to know. Because here was a poet who took poetry and parsed it into shapes never before imagined. He demonstrated clarity through restraint; he demonstrated passion through small accumulations and considerations. He negotiated a world that condemned his needs yet brought his necessities to a broad audience without upsetting them.

I am a poet. I do not claim to be in the same league as Lorca, or Cavafy. But I am glad that I've found the temerity to translate a few of their works. And I'm happy with my decision to self-publish those efforts, here on the RikVerse website. Perhaps my translations will help others find delight in these Canonical people – and if they don't? Well, at least we've got a recipe (one among many) to help people facilitate such wonder for themselves when current translations don't quite meet the mark for them.
