My mother, Anne, died earlier this month. My brother, who's arranging the funeral, asked me to write the eulogy for the service "because them vicars never get the story right" – which is true.

I've sent these words over to the Celebrant who will be leading the funeral. I have no idea whether they will make the final cut, so I shall also put them here – for posterity, as they say.

Anne Margaret Roots
8 September 1929 – 7 August 2026

Summaries are a funny thing. As people, we all have an urge to summarise the places and people around us. What is a eulogy, if not a summary of a loved one's life? What did they achieve? What did they contribute? How will we remember them?

So how can we summarise Anne? For everyone here, if told to summarise Anne with just one word, we will probably end up with a whole range of words, because Anne meant different things to different people. Not least: mother, grandmother, friend.

For some, the word 'sensible' will come to mind. For others, 'practical', or maybe 'creative' – for her solutions to problems were often unexpected. They often worked.

Ask Anne herself, as I did, and the word she circled around was 'resilient'. She strived, she survived, she overcame – because that's what she had to do, needed to do. Failing was simply not an option for her, because if she failed then everyone who had come to rely on her, love her even, would fail with her. Failure was not an acceptable outcome, for Anne.

Born Anne Margaret Last in 1929, on the outskirts of the little town of Tenterden, in Kent, Anne spent the first ten years of her life learning how to be resilient. She came from a large family. Her dad was a chef and an army man; her mother a sometime housekeeper and regular churchgoer. She had brothers and sisters whom she loved – Peter, Dorothy, John, and a little later David, and Audrey. But times were different then and children often had to grow up quickly.

At the end of her life, Anne spent some time in the elderly people's assessment centre in Tenterden. Those buildings sit on top of a hill, and Anne's room overlooked the house in the valley where she was born and grew up. Seeing the house triggered many memories for Anne, many of them happy.

The story of how she would go stealing apples from the local orchards, stuffing fruit down her knickers to maximise the amount she could take home, was a particular favourite, given the way she told it to everyone who walked into her room, with that same childlike glint of mischief in her eyes.

War broke out a week before Anne's tenth birthday. Over the course of the war, Anne attended eight different schools in Cornwall, South Wales, and Kent. She failed her eleven-plus exam, but got a result good enough for a place at Folkestone Technical School for Girls, where she completed her education. It was there that Anne learned the skills which would keep her strong over the rest of her life: book-keeping; resource management; counting pennies.

It was at school that Anne also developed her greatest passion: reading. Anne was never without a good novel at her side: often two. And she remembered every story, refused to read any book twice.

Attending school in Folkestone also meant that Anne returned to Kent after her three evacuations. During that time she lived in a house with her mother, opposite the house where Walter, her future husband, was growing up.

Unlike other places, the village of Dymchurch offered Anne something she had lacked during the war years: friends. It was in Dymchurch where Anne would learn to roller skate, trust people, laugh again.

Anne married Walter in 1949, after he completed his three years of National Service. At first the couple lived in Burmarsh where they had their first son, Paul. But within a year they were back in the village they both loved, in a council house not far from the High Street. Her daughter Shirley quickly arrived, then Andrew and Alan. Richard, their youngest son, followed a few years later.

Anne spent over seventy-five years in that house, through good times and bad. And there were many good times: kids' parties, Christmas dinners, cake baking marathons. She practised her crafts in that house. Her pottery business, painting canvases and cards for sale, making models and dollhouses – even to her last day there.

Many frocks and costumes were also sewn in that house, in particular for the Dymchurch Players theatre group in their early days. Anne never intended to end up on stage – she was far more interested in making things, not wearing them. But when circumstances conspired to make her one half of the pantomime comedy pair, Anne took to the challenge with gusto: appearing on stage for a number of years with her good friend Joyce.

If there was a defining moment in Anne's life, then it was probably when her son Alan fell ill, when he was just six. Alan was diagnosed with diabetes, and needed daily injections for the rest of his life. Anne made sure that her son not only survived, but thrived. She refused to wrap him in cotton wool, instead sending him out to play with his brothers, adventure with his friends. She was always there to pick Alan back up when he ate the wrong food, or enjoyed a pint too many. She kept her worries and fears to herself.

Money in the house was always tight: Anne found ways to make things work. For many years she worked as an agent for one of the big catalogue shopping services – Freemans. She had dozens of clients, and kept meticulous track of payments made, and payments delayed a few extra days. The commissions she earned kept her kids in school shirts and shoes.

When her youngest son started school, Anne started a new career as a shop assistant. For many years she worked in The Arcade – the sweets and bucket-and-spade shop which sits so prominently in Dymchurch High Street. She knew Bob and Roy Geering from her first year in Dymchurch, just as she knew many locals from that time. Anne loved that job – not so much for the money she earned, but rather for the chance to meet so many visitors to the village during the holiday season. Anne had a passion for pleasantries and people-watching!

In fact the only thing that stopped Anne working in shops was the Covid pandemic. Even at the age of ninety, Anne worked every Saturday afternoon in the Pilgrims Hospice bookshop in the High Street. She even learned how to use an electronic till for that job!

Not everything was good in Anne's life. Sadly her marriage to Walter broke down after many years. But Anne remained resilient. When the Geering brothers sold the Arcade, Anne moved on to working in Lewisham Council's respite care home, at the back of the village. For that job, where she started as a cleaner and ended up as the cook, Anne learned to drive a moped!

Family, however, was always central to her life. As her children grew up and moved away, she always had time in her busy schedule for the next generation. Many small visitors to the house were entertained by Anne – from drawing, painting and baking cakes to playing with the many, many fridge magnets Anne collected on her travels.

The one thing Anne feared most was retirement. She could never imagine not working, until that day came. It turned out her worries were wasted. Adult education courses led her to many new and surprising craft interests.

She also helped out with the Marsh Companions – a group of older people making time to look after and keep in contact with other older people. Beyond regular coffee mornings and bingo sessions, Anne helped to arrange group holidays both across Britain and Ireland, and further afield in Europe. Anne learned to love mountains in Austria and the Northern Lights in Norway.

And at the end of another holiday, another fridge magnet to add to her collection!

One craft Anne practised throughout her life, was knitting. All of her children learned the importance of sitting still in front of her as she unpicked outgrown sweaters, winding wool around their upheld hands. Soon enough the wool became another jumper, or scarf, or baby blanket. Anne continued knitting to the end, working with the knitting group in the Dymchurch Methodist Hall, creating tiny jumpers and hats for premature babies.

There were two things people could expect from Anne when they visited her for a chat: a good, strong cup of tea and some well-considered advice – offered, not told – to anyone who sought it. Sensible; practical; creative. But mostly resilient: Anne was a woman who didn't elbow her way into the spotlight, and never hid away when people needed her help.

Anne died in a nursing home in Hythe, after a year of illness. She was a month short of her ninety-seventh birthday. Even to the end, she made an effort to put her visitors at ease: a joke here, a reminiscence there. Such as her story of spending Christmas 1939 in London with a Jewish family whose boy her mother had taken in from Germany. In the evenings Anne and her little brother John would go carol singing, for the pleasure and the pennies. And each day, Anne would spend that money on underground train tickets, riding and walking her way around London – catching it all, savouring it all while it was there.

Anne is with her dad now, her siblings, her sons Alan and Paul. Like the elephant ornaments and trinkets she filled her house with, we shall miss her with great fondness, and abiding love.
