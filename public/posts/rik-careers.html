"So ... what do you want to be when you grow up?"

For the first time in a while, I looked into the eyes of the stranger standing in our dining room. During most of our "chat", as she called it, I had been staring at the clank of beads circling her neck. I don't like locking gazes; eyes are dangerous. Much better to stare at mouths or, in the case of this social worker, her fingers as they constantly pinched and stroked the wooden eggs strung around her creased throat.

What did I want to be? Now there was a question!

When I was five I was convinced the only job for me was 'train driver'. Thomas the Tank Engine was my favourite book: there was no other career to consider. Though driving trains wasn't enough for my young, twisted imagination; I was going to be an 'underground train driver' because tunnels were hidden, and hidden was safe.

At the age of eight all thoughts of driving anything had left my mind. My taste in books had moved on and my favourite author was now Gerald Durrell. Mr Durrell loved animals, like me. He had run wild in the never-ending Greek summers of his youth, just as I had run wild across the Romney Marshes, investigating and learning everything I could discover about my mysterious homeland. The man was a collector of animals, and had his own zoo!

It was obvious: I absolutely had to become an 'explorer of foreign lands' because foreign was not here, and not here was safe.

Later, I learned to adore the words of James Herriot. Mr Herriot was a vet, who worked and lived in a foreign land called Yorkshire with big hills and deep dales and lots of queer folks with many sick animals. I, too, with eleven years collected, was surrounded by animals – dogs, cats, rabbits, ferrets – even a mynah bird which died so quickly it forgot to loosen its grip on the perch, to be discovered that evening hanging upside down as stiff as the wood it still clung to.

Of course I had to become a 'veterinary surgeon'. For once my family approved of this idea – not that I worried about their opinions. I cared only that I'd be surrounded by animals, not people. Animals were safe!

I stared at the woman, this 'social worker' sent to "assess" me. I stared hard at her eyes – blue, I recall, though a darker blue than those of my siblings – like only a fifteen year old youth can stare.

"I enjoy studying the sciences, and I'm good with words."

"So maybe a scientist?"

"Or a novelist. Maybe both. There's no need for me to decide just yet."

The woman before me narrowed her tricksome gaze just a touch, telling me silently that, once again, I had given her the wrong answer. She was here because decisions had to be made about me: my location; my welfare; my life. My failure to supply appropriate social-worker-pre-approved answers was not welcome!

"So ... what do you want to be when you grow up?"

It's the eternal question. No child can escape it.

There are many examples of people who famously stated their life intentions at a young age and then devoted the rest of their lives to achieving those plans. Mary Shelley, inspired by her parents and their friends, declared that she would "become a writer". Oprah Winfrey worked at a radio station and determined her life plan was to be "paid for talking to people". Pablo Picasso's sole ambition, when 15, was fixed on "surpassing Raphael".

There can be no doubt that these very famous people achieved their childhood ambitions. Similarly Benjamin Franklin, Leonardo da Vinci, Nikola Tesla, Elon Musk, Taylor Swift – the list goes on – are all examples of people who had determined exactly what they wanted to do with their lives by the time they were sixteen.

There's a reason for this. Kids who can map out and then deliver a clear life plan are able to combine an unusual self-discipline with deep curiosity and inner conviction. They bring structure and imagination to their lives, they see beyond their immediate circumstances and take action to shape their destiny. Some are surrounded by mentors and enablers, while others seek out such angels as part of their plan. Through deep dedication and a brutal force of will they all turned their childhood fantasies into reality.

But for most kids, such plans are wishful thinking. Some of my school friends wanted to be 'footballers' when they grew up, or 'singers' in a band. The good looking ones hoped to become 'models'. Today's kids seem to settle for 'influencer' or, too often, just 'rich'.

We all have the capacity to daydream.

"So ... what do you want to be when you grow up?"

This time the man asking the question was my 'career advisor' – a teacher who had never taught me. I was sixteen now, studying hard in the early spring warmth and soon to be sitting my first big set of exams.

Surprisingly, perhaps, it wasn't his first question. That question had been: "So ... which Regiment are you planning to join?"

Know this: I grew up at a time in the world where boys became men and men were defined by their trade. Soldier, sailor; butcher, baker; toiler, traitor. As much as the teachers at the schools I attended might try to persuade children that hard studying could lead to great opportunities, every adult beyond the school gates knew better: stick to what your family, your community, knows; do as the world determines for you. Fit in!

When I was eleven I sat an exam and, apparently, won a place at grammar school. If Mum had opened that letter then perhaps my life would have been different. I remember the argument between my parents: "Southlands was good enough for me! It's good enough for my son," said Dad, ending the spat by throwing the letter onto the dining room fire.

And that was me. My first career was 'student' at one of very few comprehensive schools in Kent. Mum didn't give up – I was good at studenting and the school was accommodating in its uniqueness, trying hard to instill hope in its brightest students, aiming us at 'O' levels and 'A' levels and the possibility of a place at university.

My interrogator was young himself, with impressively dangerous blue eyes and a short sharp shock of black hair. Put him in camouflage fatigues and polished boots and he would have fitted right in with the Army recruiters who regularly drove their display truck through our school gates and parked up in our playground, disrupting the never-ending games of scratch-football with their enticements to enlist.

"I'm going to university," I told him, with sufficient undertone to deter further military-related interrogations. "I want to study biology".

"Why biology?"

"It's my best subject!" Which was true, but didn't answer his question. Nobody else in my family had ever gone to university; wasn't getting a degree enough?

"You Roots?" said a man sat behind a desk in a small office lined with files and boxes. "You're late! Well don't just stand there – yes, close the door!"

I stood to attention in front of the desk: "???-ZERO-PRIVATE-ROOTS-ARRR-JAY, SIR!"

"No need to scream in my face! At Ease!"

I eased.

"You know what this meeting is about, Private?"

"No, Sir."

"Hmm. My job is to fit soldiers to the most suitable trade for them. We need people with the right skills to be doing the right job. You understand?"

"Yes, Sir."

"Good. I've been through your papers – " at which point the man, whom I can only describe as a sort of archetypal Colonel running to fat, tapped on a file to one side of the desk " – and I think you have excellent prospects! We are in desperate need of radiographers, and you look a good fit for that role."

"Radiographer, Sir?"

"Yes, Roots. Radiographer."

"I'm going to be a Combat Medical Technician, Sir. That's what I joined as."

"I need to find radiographers. Why are you selling yourself short?"

"I was hoping to be an Environmental Health Technician, Sir."

"We've got too many drain sniffers ..."

"Which is why I'm happy to be a CMT until the next EHT course runs in a couple of years time ..."

"Don't be stupid! You're far too good to be a CMT – utter waste of resource!"

... The fuck?

"No. You'll be a radiographer," the fat man with a red face poking from an over-tight uniform continued. "We'll send you off to college for two years to study – no need for uniforms or saluting, it's a proper college mixing with civilian students – and we'll fast-track your promotion to Sergeant by the time you qualify!"

At this point, my guts froze. College. Students. He wanted to send me away for two fucking years to study some shit I had barely heard of before – for what? To take photos of bones?

He wanted to take away my uniform?

"I've been to college already, Sir. I'm a qualified lab technician. You can drop me into a lab and set me to work ..."

"The Army trains its own lab technicians. I've arranged an interview for you in Woolwich on Monday ..."

"... straight away, Sir ... Interview?"

"Be at the main gate at 0900 sharp Monday to pick up your transport. Wear civilian clothes. Everything has been arranged!"

"0900 Monday, Sir. But ..."

"Dismissed!"

So ... what did I want to be when I grew up?

Life is mostly a storm and offers us scant silver linings. One of the few blessings of advancing age that I have discovered is an ability to look back and answer questions that Young Me had no answer to.

For instance: what I wanted to be when I grew up, but also before, was 'safe'. So I made a plan when I was sixteen to make my desire a reality. To be safe, I needed to have complete control of my life, to not give anyone the power to make my life-decisions for me.

People believe that childhood is a time of innocence. Perhaps for many people that was a true thing. But every child has responsibilities: they need to learn how to weave a life through the complexes of their family and society; they need to learn how to negotiate the expectations that others set for (or on) them. They need to survive and, hopefully, thrive – with or without the help of good mentors and enablers.

Perhaps the expectations placed on young me were too much? My Dad arranged for my conception with one goal in mind: to keep his family from falling apart. I managed that job for fifteen years before the wheels fell off the wagon and I ended up homeless, a Ward of the Court. After their divorce finalised, and I was left with no legal guardian to look out for me for the final years of my minority, I had to grow up fast.

And yes, some of my life choices have been poor. Perhaps I could have become a great radiographer, helped discover tumours in the images I took which might have led to a precious life extended. But that was not to be: nobody takes away my hard-earned right to choose my own erratic path through life!

I did become a scientist, of sorts. I studied for a degree – three decades late – with the Open University, so my Mum could see me parade across a stage in a gown to be handed a roll of paper. I've worked as a 'policy advisor' in central government and as a 'temporary stock room assistant' in a High Street retail firm. I've pulled potatoes from claggy soil and pulled software code from my self-taught imagination.

I write. Like this blog post, which is – from a technical point-of-view – quite good.

But the one thing I forgot to include in my sixteen-year-old life plan was the most important thing of all: meet and keep good friends. I have good friends now, but it took me a long time to learn that most fundamental lesson. And that's my message for anyone who needs a take-away from this essay: good friends are as much of a life goal as any fleeting desire, or trade!

"So ... what do you really want to be when you – finally – grow up?"
