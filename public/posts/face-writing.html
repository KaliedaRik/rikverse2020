Join me, please, as we go and stare at a child asleep in his bed. You see him? He's wrapped himself in nylon sheets atop the thin mattress, which slumps in its bunk-bed frame and cuddles his form. There's a blanket tucked under one side of the foam; the other edge has been kicked free. The air is warm tonight, too warm for September, perhaps. Check out the way some strands of his summer-bleached fringe stick to the sweat across his cheek.

He's not old, you say.

He's small, I agree, short for six. Perhaps half the length of the bed when he stretches, which he seems to repeat to some stuttered rhythm only he can hear. His hands reach under the flat pillow for a moment before he rocks onto his side and scrunches a knee to his chest. So restless, this one. So quick his eyes flick beneath their lids.

Why does he fret, you wonder?

See, look here. Look inside his bronzed ear, and through it: if we push together we can break past the taut drum-skin and enter his dream ...

There he is, I say, pointing to the child who runs between statues. Some of them have color, and clothes; others are empty outlines, glowing as they fade.

There's a witch here, somewhere, I explain, no more than a long cloak and a hat, black against the shimmer light. He's trying to find her but can't see beyond the shapes of his friends and family. He needs to stop the witch touching people, leeching them.

We watch as he darts about. He seems to be defending one figure in particular. His mother, I remember. This dream is always the same: in a moment the witch will touch her – there we go. Can you see the colour collapse out of her?

You nod. Pretty! What happens next?

He has to fill her shape up with milk – don't smile! It's his nightmare, not yours. He'll spend an age pouring buckets into her outline, blanching her back to life. But he'll falter: he can always bring everything about her back to the way she needs to be – the feel of her dress, the tone of her scarf. But the face defeats him. He can never remember what his mum's face looks like.

Poor sod!

I nod. Let's hope he's lucky enough to roll himself out of bed this time, before he realises his failure: the shock of flying is much more fun to wake up to!

Some long decades later, soon after I discovered my prosopagnosia, I revised my thoughts around my most keenly remembered recurring nightmare. What if this was my six-year-old way to come to terms with my brain's failure to process faces?

I asked Dr Brad, the brain explorer who had helped to introduce the world to this new disability. He shrugged in his easy American way, his smile as wide as California surf: "It's an interesting idea!" So, probably not, then. We turned back to discussing the printouts of my MRI scans stacked on the table between us. Dr Brad was so keen to investigate people who grew up with face blindness – this condition which had been hidden in plain sight for so long – that he was willing to pay me £20 an hour for my guinea pig time.

This is the thing: I've always had this feeling that my relationship with my environment was – not wrong, rather ... different – to the way other people worked with the world. At times I've come to some rather strange conclusions to explain that feeling, that nightmare.

For instance there was the time my Mum let me stay up late to watch 'Brigadoon', the film about a Scottish village whose people wake up every hundred years to live the next day of their lives. For an over-imaginative nine-year-old this concept was revelatory: what if I only lived a day every hundred years? Perhaps that would explain why every morning, arriving at school, all the kids seemed like strangers when we gathered in our class lines? Because today's kids weren't the same as yesterday's kids. New actors for each friend, if you like.

It took me a good few weeks to grow out of that delusion. But it was a fun explanation while it lasted.

Mostly, however, my unknown disability was a minor abrasion to my life. More impactful was the way I learned to lie my way through school eye tests, leaving my short-sightedness undiagnosed until I was fourteen. I was a vain child keen to keep clear from the schoolyard taunts reserved for those who wore glasses.

And the condition did give me some unexpected superpowers, now easily explained. For instance I was always better than the other kids at guessing which teachers and dinner ladies were patrolling the playground, even at a distance with only the backs of their heads on display. I developed coping strategies – the cut of a coat, the stomp in a gait – which normal people rarely notice until told to look for such details.

It's strange how things balance out.
