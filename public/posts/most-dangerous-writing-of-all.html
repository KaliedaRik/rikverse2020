I didn't know my Uncle Jim well. He was a lot older than my Dad and lived far away in a magical place called Ascot, which was quite close to a big castle, a fantasy lake and a tame safari park with baboons and lions and stuff.

I was seven or eight when my parents drove me to Ascot to spend a week with Jim and Claire. Their kids were grown up and gone so it was just me and the old couple in their immaculate house. The holiday was over within two days. Aunty Claire's corgi – of course it had to be a corgi; corgis are royal! – savaged me that first evening, after which I became severely uncooperative, refusing all food, drink and comfort. Dad collected me the next evening; no words were spoken on that long night drive home.

And that was that. Five decades passed without me giving much of any thought to any of my parents' siblings and their spouses. Their histories were not my history, thus not my problem.

A few months ago, on Facebook, a cousin mentioned in passing that Uncle Jim had written a memoir before he died. Other cousins joined the conversation, chatting about the awful circumstances surrounding my Dad's family while they were growing up.

I asked where I could buy, or find, this book; promises were made and soon enough I possessed what turned out to be 39 pages of tightly typed, paragraph free recollection.

As I started reading the stapled A4 sheets, I had trouble keeping my mind to the task. This was not "good writing" – but family history interests me so I pushed through the tight-bricked wall of text until I stopped seeing the words, heard once more a long-forgotten voice ...

The days shortened and the day came when we werent going to school, as usual, We were going to stay with some other children, for a little while, Could we still see grandma yes sometimes, My litt le brain couldnt think of anything else to say, We had some bags packed and with the new baby we were taken in Mr Garraways old trap to a big Brick house at the other side of Bracknall. There was a big arched gateway and a man in a little room . We waited here for a little while and then we were taken to another place. with 2 beds and some other furniture. and red lino on the floor, We sat around talking and then a man and a lady brought us some tea and foodon a tray. ... Ignore the punctuation, the erratic capitalisation, the (rare) misspelling. Forget the missing line breaks. Just read it. Then read it again.

In a bunch of run-along words Uncle Jim described one of the worst days of his life. The day when his family, long abandoned by their father, finally became destitute and had to move into the local Workhouse.

I have a diploma in Creative Writing. All the learning I did to get through that course is for nothing, compared to the shocking impact of his tightly remembered details.

Everyone has a story in them – as cliches go, it's more true than most.

My introduction to "trauma writing" or, as I've come to call it more recently, "trauma porn", came through reading confessional poetry, in particular Robert Lowell, John Berryman and Anne Sexton.

I like the work of these poets. I like in particular the necessary bravery it took for them to ink their words onto paper and persuade business folks to risk publishing them. Breaking taboos about acceptable subject matter was a critical intervention back in the 1950s and 1960s: mental health; sexual assault; childhood deprivation – these are all incredibly important topics that needed to be pushed into the spotlight. Without the work of the early confessionals I doubt we'd be in a place today, sixty years later, where everyone is allowed to share their experiences of life – hopefully in a safe and accepting space.

But now, six decades on, are we really in a safe space? How much current trauma writing is published as exemplars to truth, or as drivers for profit? Do I learn from these works, or just enjoy them: another form of entertainment in our over-saturated infotainment space?

I thought my answers to such questions were settled. Until I read my Uncle's memoir. His clumsy words took an axe to my casual cynicism. By the time I had read to the end of his staple-bound story I knew with certainty that these words absolutely had to exist, deserved to exist!

People should know Uncle Jim's story, I decided. Perhaps I could tidy his words? Introduce the narrative to the concept of paragraph breaks and considered punctuation? Use my hard-earned skills to bring his hard-wrung life to the attention of the wider world?

"No!"

My immediate response was visceral, judgmental. To edit my uncle felt ... wrong. This was his story, told in his way. He wished us, his wider family, to learn our history: I can't imagine him choosing to type out his unvarnished emotions for a stranger's consumption. He kept a tidy house: he had his pride!

I think maybe his story has a place in a local museum or library, alongside similar works such as letters from the Western Front. Places where people have been trained to curate such historical items. They don't belong in an Amazon listing with its price for lurid admission.

Because memoir writing isn't dangerous in itself – though writing memoir can be its own trauma to those who attempt it without good support systems in place (as I learned, and continue to learn, myself).

Memoir deserves more than commodification. Prove me wrong, I dare you!
