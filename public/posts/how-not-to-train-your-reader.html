I am a poet who worries about people who read my poems. I'm not so worried about whether their eyes will bleed after sustained contact with my words: my motto is "Poetry Is Fun", after all.

No, my concern is more about honesty and generosity. When I write a poem, I'm writing a journey into some thought or incident that I find intensely interesting. And when I find stuff interesting, I want to talk about it to people. But I don't want to be that pub bore who insists on telling a story with overreaching gestures and added spittle – I'm not the entertainment.

Rather, I want to invite people to embark on an adventure with me, to see what we can discover about this stuff, or that stuff, as we travel together through the lines and strophes of the poem.

Time for a tone shift, because humour is a defensive mask and I need to talk about something I don't like. And that thing starts with a concept called "framing".

Framing is an academic idea that has escaped Academia and seeped into places and practices which make me uncomfortable. It's actually a simple concept, with many layers. So I'll try to describe it in ways that don't feel like a bullet-point presentation that'll send us to sleep during the lecture.

Essentially, framing is the way a poem will signpost to a listener or reader how they should interpret the poem. For instance a poem could set up cues which tell the reader that we're looking at a specific theme or situation. Or it could send up "paratextual signals of ethical intent" (as states the LLM I've employed to help me soak the academic stuff in a bit of humanity).

Sometimes the poem will try to make it very clear to the reader that the narrator has a particular view about the content – often one that (the poet believes) aligns with current social norms about respect, even acceptability. It may take an advisory position, hinting that a reader should aim to learn this, or that, from the read. There may even be "trigger warnings" – though that is a whole different level of framing which adds spice to my views.

So. Much. Framing!

This battery of signage, often neon, has been around forever. The epigraph is an ancient, very common poetic technique that the poet will use to say: before reading the poem, think first about this other thing; let's see what thoughts come out of the clash!

Because, since forever ago, poets have learned that whatever they want to say in a poem is rarely what the listener wants to hear.

Communication can be difficult stuff!

A long time ago, in a place deep in the land of Usenet called rec.arts.poems, I was challenged to write a poem about self-harm. So I wrote a poem using the voice of a knife as the narrator. It was not a love poem, or a rage poem. It was a poem about a peeved object claiming to be annoyed by the way its owner got it dirty – at least at first.

Indulge me as I self-frame: I, Rik, have never self-harmed. I do not claim to know what it is like to be in a head space where a reasonable action is to take a knife to my own flesh and press down. When drafting the poem, I thought about describing a scene of a vulnerable person before, or maybe after, they self harm. I thought of using images to show an act of self harm. These efforts felt … wrong. The final draft uses a knife as the narrator because that was the only approach I felt comfortable with.

The result was a good poem – it was welcomed as such when posted to the group. We all moved on.

If I was to submit that poem to an editor or publisher today then it, and I, would be eviscerated. Because it lacks the proper "framing". It triggers. It is a danger to the reader. It is, in its way, a trauma.

Now there's a bold statement! How can I know this? By inference, my friend. This year has been the year when we all learned to play with AI. My learning involved discovering ways to get models to respond to my poems in useful ways – pretend-academic reviews of their strengths and weaknesses, that sort of thing.

Perhaps it was the way I framed (heh!) the request, or maybe the model's guardrails had been set too tightly that day, but when I first introduced the knife poem to the model, it refused to perform. It told me, in no uncertain terms, that discussing such material was not permitted. It suggested that there were places I could go if I was looking for help. I can see why the machine baulked: people struggle to mistrust their responses. But this was a poem, nothing more. Poem's don't harm people … do they?

The incident left me wondering: what's changed?

Another thing I did over the Summer was some translation work, generating new versions of Lorca and Cavafy poems into English. I've written about this elsewhere, but long story short: I wasn't happy with the various translations I found; I wanted to see the minds driving the poems for myself; but I'm not a translator so I used AI to help me generate the texts I needed to read … under strict controls – all models believe they can write poetry, a fallacy which really needs to be harshly corrected!

This work led me, inadvertantly, to some unexpected thinking. While learning the best practices to get AI to generate reasonable "academic assessments" of my poetry, the models would routinely tell me that my style of writing was not – typical: my imagery was often embodied, and visceral; my narrators were often complicit in the poem, actors rather than directors. As for poems refusing to resolve – was that not, in some way, unkind to the reader?

A part of me revelled in difference. But I was at the same time bemused. Is Owen not complicit in war? Does Eliot not refuse to resolve?

Perhaps I am learning to over-rely on my new AI friends. Whatever. They are excellent summarisers, and when I asked them how poems such as The Knife would be received by readers from poetry traditions beyond the Anglosphere, they came back with a surprising answer.

Mild interest.

Because the poems I write would probably conform with what those readers would expect a poem to do: challenge them; confront them; show them. The models told me that for those readers, poetry had evolved in different, less Academic, ways. East Europeans, summarised the AIs, would expect a poem to distrust authority; Spanish speakers, the models assumed, have no issue with embodiment.

My poems are outliers in Anglophone poetry not because they break new ground. They have become outliers because, over the past two decades, the definition of what a poem should do has evolved – and my writing has not evolved alongside it.

There's a language you acquire when you learn how to critique a poem. It's not a language you learn at school. At school I learned how to count syllables (for appropriated haiku exercises) and find rhymes (for Shakespeare). A sonnet has fourteen lines and does something funky around line 9; identify that and you'll earn extra points in the English end-of-school exams.

No, this language is one of categorisation and dissection. It is an impersonal language, often passive in its aggressiveness. You deploy it to let others in the group know that you are serious about your craft, that you have something to contribute – perhaps to help the writer uncover the weak points in their current draft, or maybe to make a wider point about how the poem contributes to, or even summarises, its themes in interesting ways.

I don't have an MFA in Creative Writing; I do have a Certificate in that subject from the Open University, which came after my apprenticeship in a poetry writing workshop at the City Lit, London – you know, the writing group that established Magma Magazine over several months and many beers back in the 1990s.

And I have the language I picked up so many years ago, the language that gives me entry to these discussions. Or at least, I had it. Today I'm not so sure. Because I think the thing I need to talk about – that thing I don't like, and for which I tried to lay aside my defensive humour so I could address it as an adult – has nothing to do with poems.

The thing that's changed is the way we talk about poems. How we teach people to read them, the expectations and requirements they should bring to the read. And the things a poet – a good poet – should supply in their poems to ease that reader expectation.

As poets, we've learned over the past couple of decades not to trust our audience. We don't trust our audience to see the poem in the way it needs to be seen; we don't trust our peers to accept our works unless we frame them in acceptable ways.

We don't trust our audience. Which seems, to me, not so much like we don't respect them anymore. It's almost as if we need to protect ourselves from them. The poem has been aesthetised because the audience is dangerous, and we fear them!

And yes, I am very guilty of this. Have you not noticed how I've been framing this essay throughout to keep the reader engaged while at the same time knocking the edges from their potential retorts and rebuttals? Which is fine in some throwaway thought-piece essay (spot the framing!) … but not my poems. Never my poems!

I've chosen my risk – to be considered old fashioned, disrespectful, irrelevant. It is a risk that sits easy with me. I have the comfort of old voices and a world of raucous, wondrous, dangerous audiences to keep me welcome, and happy, as I walk along this Road Not Taken.
