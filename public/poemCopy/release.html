What does it even mean, to die?
Dymchurch beach disappears daily:
twice salted for preservation,
twice drained to leave long strands
of flatlined water. Sometimes the ribs
of swamped, swallowed ships unbury,
revealed as the moon and sun see fit.

Even before you married the man
you learned his danger, his will to pull
you under the water just a game to him,
a teenage courtship among summer friends.

Now you are dust, we have discussed
between us and agreed not to scatter
you over waves. You taught your children
to work the sands, to harvest treasures.
You were born on chalk, not grit or mud.

But what can it even mean, to die?
It's not enough, the how: that breathing
was a habit you just put aside one day
to leave us stunned, yet relieved.

I lost you when you forgot why you feared
the sea. When the struggle for breath
became your routine, not a recall
of how you met our father. Like the moon
pulled a tide over your mind: I lost you
many times as the who of you shuffled
between the long strands of your stories.

"We'll scatter her over the Downs,"
my brother decides, "perhaps next year,
when spring arrives?" I smile: of course.
The moon has no voice; it never did.
It is better to take you back to chalk,
to those storied hills baked by long sun,
and together shake you free of us.
