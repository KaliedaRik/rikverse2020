This space is nice, bright and roomy. The accommodation at the rehabilitation centre had been more cramped – barely wide enough to fit the bed and a chair. Which in itself was superior to the hospital ward with its faint smell of boiled cabbage and old confusion. Mum has been having an adventure these past few weeks.

Before visiting, I had taken time to wander around the town that hosted this assessment centre. A tidy town deep in the Kent countryside: isolated enough to avoid the normal high street shops, giving it some quirk to its character. The low banks of clouds gave the large church some stout mettle – I remember Mum telling me how the Germans had kept their bombs away from this part of Bomb Alley, so useful had the massive spire been for signposting the planes towards London.

The room has a large window that captures a panoramic pastoral of trees in their autumn finery staking the rich green grass to the rolling chalk downs.

"I took some photos on my way here. You want to see them?"

"That would be nice," Mum agrees.

"The town's changed a lot since you were last here, I expect."

She nods, reaches out for me to pass her my phone. "I haven't been here for eighty years or more. We moved away before the war broke out. I was only ten when we left."

I pull up the photos and hand her the device. She struggles with the navigation at first, accidentally zooming, or inviting the AI to offer unwanted interfaces. She can't sit up comfortably, so I go onto my knees so our faces can be closer as I show her how to slide a finger across the slick of glass at just the right speed for easy transitions.

She enjoys the little train station. "I remember when I was six my Mother gave me some money. 'Go to the station and get a ticket to ...' I can't remember where, it was a couple of stops. 'You can spend the holidays with your uncle Bill.' So I had to walk myself down that big hill and wait for the train. Then at the other end I had to ask for directions but the station master sent someone to get uncle."

I know this story. It's one of her more sticky tales, thus gets mentioned whenever talking about railways. I could quote the punchline along with her, but respect keeps me smiling instead.

"And when he turned up, he said: 'Where's your bag?' but Mother hadn't packed me a bag. I had to share clothes with my cousins for the whole month!"

We laugh at her reminiscence. Mum's Mother was good at getting rid of her at every opportunity – holidays with relatives, evacuations with schools to Cornwall or Wales. Staying with Uncle Bill was one of her happier memories.

Flick, flick ... she's becoming an expert with the device now. And then she slows, takes more time to look at the photos. She's reached the shots I took of the church and churchyard.

"What's up?" I ask, when her finger stops moving.

"That's near to where we buried him."

"Who?"

"Richard."

"I'm Richard, Mum. I don't think I'm in the churchyard!"

"Not you, idiot."

"Who, then?"

"My baby brother. He was only 6 weeks when he died."

"Oh," I said. This was new information to me. I remember faint rumours of some stillbirths in the family tree, but never any names attached to them.

Mum's in deep memory now. A new thought, perhaps, triggered by the churchyard. Or maybe made up? People don't live to be ninety six without suffering some damage to the memory banks.

"They wouldn't let Mother bury him in the churchyard. He hadn't been christened. So Dad went and dug a grave at the back of the church, just the wrong side of the wall, where no one would notice. He'd take me for a walk, sometimes, and we'd go and tidy the spot ..."

Outside, I can see leaves ruffle at a soft touch of breeze. The sheep have wondered out of sight to graze elsewhere, and the clouds are breaking as the afternoon dies, letting through a wash of blue.

"So I'm named after my uncle Richard?"

"Yes," she says as she flicks to the next picture. A casual yes, not remembering that she had always told me my name was just a name she liked. "Is that the high street? I don't recognise any of these shops!"
