In this dream I convene my familiar ghosts
for an unwanted task, for I must set them
around the long table that looks over
the gardens of eternal June. I watch them
arrive from my comfort corner. The father
of my mother always dresses in white suits
beneath round glasses. He presents as old
because I only know him as the man of books
who lived with my cut-glass 'aunt' a spit
from the Sussex shore. He boils his onions
for he was a good chef; his love-chosen wife
drinks a shot of hydrochloric acid
after every meal, by order of doctors
who did their best. There is a distance
between my grandfathers, an acre's worth
of market garden, for my father's father
is always a gardener to me. His peasant
demeanour matches his jacket, his hat.
He comes with a dance of his own ghosts,
the revenants of the soldiers he bayonetted
in the chilly, hilly mud of Passchendaele.

I spot my grandmothers walking through grass.
They each follow their own rabbit trail.
My table is long, but straight: I need
four heads to it to keep these ancestors
agreeable. My brother who died too young
chuckles at my worry: "The entertainment
will be cheap, tonight," he reminds me.
"They married the wrong women," I agree.
It is a wicked game I play, to imagine
outcomes if the cook had married the homemaker
who survived workhouses after the killing man
deserted her and his young boys for tropical seas
when the guns lost their kick. But it is what it
has to be; my mother liked her mother
in law far more than the woman who grew her.
I watch as the woman of musts sits to rest
on her heel-flattened path; she would walk
with Jesus but Jesus declined my invite
and she refuses to enter alone. "You knew her
nine years longer than me," I tell my brother,
"You go fetch the woman. She can dine apart,
off the back of any man keen to bend for her."

My room is sparse in this skull space, cool
to the midsummer breeze which puffs a tease
of nets through open windows, missing doors.
I have many relatives, most barely recalled.
They all expect me to know them, to care
for their names. But I am here as one dreamer,
forgetful and selfish to the history we write
together. And this party is not for them:
I have new places to set in my wake-drained sight.
Five fresh chairs to push into the table:
brother and sister; her son, my nephew, heart full
and failed. I spot my favourite cousin, culled
by pneumonia this last Easter, walking across
the noise-damp rugs with her arm at the waist
of a shade whose nerve-etched face I never wanted
to welcome here. Still I smile: that face
deserves all my smiles, even the ones
I withheld when we both breathed June air.
She carries a secret bundle. Her baby brother,
lost at six weeks, buried unchristened
unseen at night by her white-clad dad,
just the wrong side of the church wall.
The infant whose name I carried, unknown,
for so long. "Where shall I put him?"
my smile collector asks. "Pop him on the table,
Mum," I say. "He is the centrepiece, for now."
