July, beyond school, and the forever wind
that moves Channel salts across the Marsh
has stilled. Lunch is whatever soft berries
we can lift from the hedge that smothers
the banks of the ditch. We chat destinations.

This is a hot sun, and the Marsh sweats
in its emerald fields: wheat, potatoes;
a first flush of golden rape. I have a word
new to my growing idiolect. As we halt
on the clear side of the road, I test it:

"Why would anyone abort a baby, Mum?"

You point at a spray of blackberries, fresh
above my head and I reach up to pluck them,
add them to the bag knotted to my belt.
You don't look at me. From here even I
can see the spire of the Burmarsh church.

The water in the maze of ditch is stagnant,
the pungent muds releasing their spice
of rot as the hard sun in its blue sky
dries the Levels. There's roadkill ahead,
a rabbit flattened at dawn. I jump past
its fur, hating the sandals you still
make me wear yet keen to keep them clean.

"Why didn't you abort me?"

You reach out and circle my shoulders
with a strong arm, draw me close. Our steps
match now, my legs will soon reach yours
in length. You instruct me in regulations
and tell me to check the road for traffic.

"If you had the chance, would you have?"

There's no thought in the hug you offer
after the word left your mouth. Hidden
in the great dock leaves, a pheasant
with gold in its wings shifts after worms.
The gentle claps of Hythe rifles roll
through the still, hot air as we walk.
