It is not my town, my home. This is mother
territory: her birth, her church, her walk
along the streets and down the steep chalk hill
to the station and its steaming trains ready
to move her elsewhere for summer, or safety.

How do I know this place? Why does first-stepping
from the bus and across the town's wide paths
feel like a lost scarf loose-wound around my neck
to defer the wind's chill cut? A wonder shivers
my nape: what if this Tenterden air knows me?

Old people with their supported steps stop up
the easy route. I need to weave polite apologies
as I push the length of the high street, no time
to stop at shop fronts, watch as keepers build
their Christmas displays: models and stencils.

She loved me, back then, the tall-steeple church
murmurs. I felt her watching her brothers play seek
between my barrel graves before they went to choir.
I slow to consider its heavy stacks of stone,
pull out my phone to snap images, test this claim.

I'll show her you through this, I promise the town.
Maybe she can share you with me. Maybe she'll smile.

She's been losing memories, I tell the dead-leaf
verge that leads to her. She's not lost in time;
she knows you, plays you well, the bronze verge
suggests. Her pantry of stories lost some shelves
is all: there's nourishment in her for any who ask.

Still, who could guess that her authority would assess
her fitness to live beyond its bounds in a room
overlooking the house she arrived in? A laugh
of a chance, my brother said, yet here we are: me
and her newfound stash of child-in-sepia stories.

It is not my town, my home, I proclaim to sheep mid-cud
in their green sheer of field which hides a rust of old
metalled tracks in its fold, but I'll take her delight.
I wonder, as I step, what memories I've packed tight
in the back of my toy cupboard, safe for unsafe days?
